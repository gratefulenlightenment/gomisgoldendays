Caring for a senior dog

Is my senior dog too cold?

By Gomi's human — written from daily life with Gomi, now twelve (@gomithesuperyorkie), not a vet's office · Last updated November 16, 2026 · about an 8-minute read

Halfway round the block your old dog stops, lifts one front paw, then the other, and looks back toward the house. Small, older dogs lose heat faster than they used to, and shivering, slowing down, lifting their paws, and turning for home are how they tell you. Put her coat on before you open the door, shorten the walk, and move her bed out of the draft tonight.

Get her warm and call the clinic now if: she becomes dull, confused, or very weak; her breathing is slow; she collapses or cannot stand; or her ear tips, paws, or tail tip look pale, gray, or discolored, swell, or hurt when touched. On the way, wrap her in warm blankets. Do not rub skin that may be frostbitten, and never use hot water. This article is general information, not a diagnosis.

Why an older dog feels the cold more

Every dog can get too cold. Some lose the battle much sooner, and an older small dog has several reasons at once. They are worth knowing, because each one points to a different fix.

A small body loses heat fast

Cornell's canine health center puts it plainly: small dogs have a harder time staying warm than larger breeds (Cornell, winter safety tips). The reason is geometry. A small body has a lot of skin for the amount of body inside it, so heat escapes quickly. A small dog is also close to the ground. Her belly and chest pass just above cold pavement, wet grass, or snow the whole walk.

Age, weight, and illness

The same Cornell page lists older animals, underweight animals, and those with other medical conditions as more likely to have trouble staying warm, and AAHA names senior pets and small breeds among the most sensitive to cold (AAHA, cold weather safety). Many older dogs also carry less muscle and less fat than they did in their prime; muscle makes heat, and fat holds it in. A dog who has started to feel the cold much more than last winter is telling you something about her body, not just about the weather.

A thin or freshly clipped coat

Cornell notes that short and thinly coated breeds do not tolerate cold as well as dogs with thick double coats, and it advises against clipping a dog's coat too short in winter. A long-haired small dog who is kept in a short trim for easy grooming has, in effect, swapped her winter coat for a summer one.

Stiff joints and slippery ground

Cold also costs an older dog in a less obvious way. Dogs with arthritis often struggle in cold weather because ice and snow make every step less stable, Cornell says. A dog who is bracing against a slip walks stiffly and tires sooner, which means more time standing still in the cold.

What you can do tonight and this week

Most of what keeps an older dog warm happens before you go out and after you come back. These six changes cost little and start working the same day.

1. Dress her before the door opens

For smaller, older, or thin-coated dogs, Cornell says a jacket or sweater helps keep them warm, and the AVMA suggests one for any dog with a short coat or who seems bothered by the cold (AVMA, cold weather hazards). Put it on indoors, while she is still warm, so she starts the walk with heat to keep rather than heat to make up.

A coat only works while it is dry. If it gets soaked in rain or wet snow, take it off as soon as you are home and towel her dry, and keep a second one so the next walk does not start in a damp coat.

2. Choose a coat that actually fits

A coat that rubs, slips, or traps her legs will make a walk worse. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center lists what to look for, and it is a good checklist to take shopping (Riney Canine Health Center, January 2026):

  • Fits well, neither tight nor loose.
  • Allows a full range of motion in the legs and shoulders.
  • Feels comfortable and does not irritate the skin.
  • Lets her relieve herself easily.
  • Goes on and comes off easily, which matters for a dog with stiff joints.
  • Stays fastened while she moves.

AAHA adds that it should cover her chest and back, and that a dog who has never worn clothes should be introduced to them gradually. For a small dog, a coat that covers most of the underside also keeps snow from balling up on her belly and chest, Cornell notes.

3. Shorten the walk and move it to the warmest hour

The AVMA's advice is simply to shorten walks in very cold weather, and AAHA suggests short, frequent walks rather than long outings when it is extremely cold. Early morning, before the sun is up, is usually the coldest part of the day, and a midday walk can be the same distance in very different conditions. Our guide to how far a senior dog should walk explains how to judge the right length by how she moves the next morning, and cold mornings are where that test matters most. If you do walk in the dark, Cornell recommends something reflective and a flashlight so drivers can see you both.

4. Know the five signs that she has had enough

Dogs say they are cold well before they are in danger. Riney's list of signs is worth learning, because each one is easy to read as stubbornness:

  • Shivering.
  • Walking more slowly, or refusing to walk.
  • Trying to turn back toward home or the car.
  • Lifting her feet off the ground, often one after another.
  • Whining, or stopping and refusing to go on.

Any of these means head home now, not at the end of the loop. Once she is in, wrap her in a blanket and let her settle somewhere warm. The AVMA advises contacting the clinic if a dog is still shivering or is extremely lethargic after being out in the cold.

5. Move her bed out of the draft

Many older dogs spend far more of the day asleep than out walking, so the bed matters more than the coat. Riney recommends a warm bed or blanket placed out of drafty areas. In practice that means away from outside doors, under-door gaps, and windows, and off bare tile or stone if you can manage it. A blanket she can burrow under lets her decide how warm she wants to be. Some dogs also benefit from wearing a sweater indoors, Riney adds.

Heaters need care. Riney advises keeping dogs, and their beds and blankets, about three feet from fireplaces, stoves, and space heaters, and not leaving a dog alone near one. An old dog who moves slowly or sleeps deeply may not shift away from heat in time.

6. Check and wipe her paws after every walk

Cornell recommends wiping or washing paws after winter walks to remove salt, de-icers, and the snowballs that collect between the toes, and the AVMA adds the legs and belly. Longer-haired dogs may collect ice and snow on their feet, and Cornell suggests trimming the fur on the feet to help prevent it (Cornell CVM). While you are there, look for cracked pads or redness between the toes. Snowballs between the toes can be uncomfortable, Cornell notes, so if she starts limping on a snowy walk, check her feet first.

What we did with Gomi

Gomi is a Yorkshire terrier of about eleven pounds, and she has worn sweaters for years. The photo at the top of this page is from November 2019, and there are older ones. Another, also from 2019, shows her tucked under a strawberry-patterned blanket in her bed. Photos also show her both long-coated and with her body clipped short, as in the one further down this page. By Cornell's reasoning above, a short clip means a thinner winter coat.

Our walks are by the river before dawn, which in winter is about the coldest time of day we could pick. When it gets properly cold, we wait until the sun is up or keep the walk short, and we turn back as soon as she shows the signs in the list above. Her coat goes on just before we go out and comes off once we are back inside. After a short trim, Gomi wears it for longer, sometimes indoors as well. Whatever the weather, the walk still ends the way it always has, with a treat afterwards.

At night she makes her own adjustments. On cold nights she gets under her blanket and stays there.

Over the last couple of winters I have come to think Gomi feels the cold more than she used to. I cannot prove it. She is older, she rests more, and some days she limps, and any of those could make a cold morning harder for her.

What to write down before the vet visit

If she seems to feel the cold more this winter than last, a few notes make that easy to discuss:

  • The walks where she showed cold signs, with the time, the weather, and how long you had been out.
  • How quickly she warmed up once she was inside.
  • Stiffness on cold mornings compared with mild ones.
  • Where she chooses to sleep, and whether she has started burrowing or shivering at night.
  • Any cracked pads, sore paws, or a limp after walks on salted or icy ground.

What only your vet can do

Hypothermia and frostbite are treated in the clinic. Warming a very cold dog safely takes monitoring, and Cornell notes that the extent of frostbite can take a few days to become clear, so a paw or ear that looks better tonight still deserves a check.

If she has started to feel the cold far more than she used to, that is worth raising at her next check-up. Several medical conditions can make it harder for a dog to stay warm, and blood work and an exam are how they are found. Morning stiffness that is worse in the cold is also worth reporting. Pain relief for sore joints has to be prescribed; never reach for human painkillers. More on reading those signs is in 7 subtle signs your dog is in pain.

Frequently asked questions

How can you tell if your dog has been in the cold for too long?

Shivering, walking more slowly or refusing to go on, lifting her paws off the ground in turn, whining, and turning back toward home all mean it is time to go in. Once she is inside, warm her with blankets. If she becomes dull or confused, breathes slowly, or is still shivering and very sluggish after warming up, call the clinic straight away.

How do I know if my dog is cold at night?

A cold dog curls into a tight ball, tucks her nose in, shivers, or keeps moving to find a warmer spot or to burrow under something. Move her bed out of drafts and off cold tile, give her a blanket she can get under, and keep the bed about three feet from any heater. Night restlessness in an old dog can have other causes too, so note when it happens.

Does my dog need to wear something when we walk outside and it's cold?

Small dogs, older dogs, and dogs with thin or short coats benefit most, according to Cornell's canine health center. Choose a coat that covers her chest and back, fits without rubbing, lets her move freely, and lets her relieve herself easily. Large, thick-coated dogs often need less, but every dog is vulnerable in freezing weather.
