Caring for a senior dog

Is my dog too old for surgery?

By Gomi's human — written from daily life with Gomi, now twelve (@gomithesuperyorkie), not a vet's office · Last updated November 16, 2026 · about an 8-minute read

The vet says your old dog needs an operation, and the first thing you think is that she will not wake up from it. Age alone rarely settles that. What settles it is how well her heart, kidneys, and liver are working right now, and whether the surgery is planned or an emergency. So the first thing to ask is what the pre-surgery checks showed, and only then how old is too old.

Go in today, without waiting to make up your mind, if: an unspayed female is suddenly drinking far more, vomiting, listless, swollen in the belly, or has any discharge; or a dog who has just come home from surgery cannot be woken easily, is still wobbly after 24 hours, refuses food and water for more than 12 hours, cries for more than an hour, or is struggling to breathe. This article is general information, not a diagnosis.

Why age is not the number that decides

Anesthesia is the part most of us fear, and it is where age has been measured. A study of 157,318 dogs in ordinary UK veterinary practices found that 0.14% died within two weeks from causes related to anesthesia or sedation, about 14 dogs in every 10,000 (Shoop-Worrall et al., Veterinary Anaesthesia and Analgesia, 2022). Dogs over nine had roughly thirteen times the odds of young adults. That is a comparison against dogs aged six months to a year and a half. It is not a death rate for old dogs.

The same analysis found that health on the day moved the odds too. Before a procedure the anesthesia team gives each patient a health grade from 1 to 5, called ASA status. Against dogs graded 1 or 2, a grade of 3 carried about five times the odds, and grades 4 and 5 about nineteen times.

AAHA's senior care guidelines put the conclusion in one line: "Advanced age alone is not a contraindication to general anesthesia" (AAHA, 2023). Age is not a disease, but disease is more common in old animals, and an old body is slower to correct a fall in blood pressure or body temperature while it is under (AAHA anesthesia guidelines, 2020). The birthday stands in for those things. The pre-surgery checks measure them directly.

Three more things change the picture for a particular dog.

  • Planned or emergency. The UK study found higher odds for urgent procedures than for planned ones. An emergency removes the time to steady a sick dog first.
  • Size. AAHA notes that very small dogs lose heat faster and are harder to intubate and monitor, so for a small old dog warmth is a safety measure.
  • The hours afterwards. In a worldwide study of 55,022 anesthetics, 81% of the anesthesia-related deaths came after the procedure, not during it (Redondo et al., Veterinary Record, 2023). Waking up and the first two days are part of the anesthetic, and some of that time is spent in your house.

What you can do before and after the operation

None of this replaces the clinic's own instructions. These are the parts that fall to the owner, in the order you will meet them.

1. Ask what the checks showed

Ask for results you can write down, not for reassurance. These questions tend to get useful answers:

  • What did the exam and blood work show, and when was the blood drawn? AAHA gives no fixed rule, but treats three to six months as a reasonable window for a healthy animal with normal results, and repeats the tests if anything was off.
  • Were her kidneys and heart checked specifically? Those are the two the senior guidelines single out.
  • What health grade did she get, and what is behind it? The grade tells you how the team sees her overall health, and the reason behind it tells you what they will be watching.
  • Who watches her while she is under and while she wakes up? The senior guidelines describe a dedicated, trained technician whose job is the monitoring.
  • How will she be kept warm? This matters most for small dogs.
  • What is the pain plan, in the hospital and at home?
  • What happens if we wait three months? The honest comparison is surgery against the untreated problem, not surgery against nothing.
  • Is there anything else she needs that could be done under the same anesthetic?

2. Hand over a one-page history

The anesthesia team plans around what you tell them. AAHA's guide for owners lists what they will ask, and it is easier to write it down at the kitchen table than to remember it at the front desk: her energy and how far she walks before tiring, any coughing or change in breathing, any vomiting, changes in appetite, how she was after any earlier anesthetic, and every medication, supplement, and over-the-counter product she gets, with what was given in the last 24 hours.

3. Set up the house before you leave for the clinic

You will come home carrying a groggy dog, and that is the wrong moment to start moving furniture. Put her bed on the floor in a warm, quiet room where you can see her. Block the stairs and the sofa. Lay a rug or a mat over any slick floor between the bed and the door. Put water within a few steps, and for a small dog add a blanket she can get under, because a body coming out of anesthesia holds heat poorly. AAHA's advice for the first 24 hours is no stairs, no jumping, and no wandering outside alone.

4. Know what the first day normally looks like

Most of what frightens people on the first night is expected. She may be drowsy or confused for 12 to 24 hours, though she should still wake when you touch her. She may walk unsteadily for a few hours, eat less for a day, and whine more than usual. A mild cough from the breathing tube can last a day or two, and many dogs pass no stool for 24 to 48 hours (AAHA discharge guidance). The signs that are not expected are the ones in the warning box near the top of this page, plus a cough that turns wet or keeps getting worse.

When the clinic says she can eat, offer a small portion of her usual food. The first night is not the time for new treats or food from your plate, even if she looks at you the way she will look at you.

5. Keep a short log until the recheck

Write down the time of each dose, what she ate and drank, and when she peed and passed stool. Take one photo of the wound each morning in the same light. At the recheck you will be asked all of this, and a tired memory is a poor witness. The photos show change that you cannot see from one day to the next.

Ask for the rest period in writing, too. For a spay, the American College of Veterinary Surgeons describes about two weeks of house rest with no running, jumping, or rough play (ACVS). Other operations differ, so go by the sheet you are sent home with.

6. Watch for pain and report it

Old dogs are quiet about pain, and after surgery the quiet can pass for sleepiness. Look at the things she does without thinking: whether she shifts position, whether she gets up to drink, whether she will lie on the side that was operated on, whether she flinches when you reach toward the wound. AAHA tells owners not to ignore distressed crying that goes on for more than 30 to 60 minutes. If you see any of this, phone the clinic and describe it. The pain plan can be changed, and nobody at the clinic can change it without knowing.

What we did with Gomi

Gomi has been under general anesthesia for two very different reasons, both at around eleven. The first was never a decision. I had been away on a trip, and Gomi had stayed with my mother-in-law. My wife went to bring her home, and by every account Gomi was running around as usual until she came through our door. Then she saw me, and she started to limp. She crept across the floor looking straight at me. That was the only sign there was. I picked her up and we went to the hospital.

It was pyometra, an infected uterus, and they told us it was a serious emergency. They operated the same day. When the uterus came out, it was close to bursting. I came very near to losing her to something that had shown itself as a limp.

I have thought about why it showed when it did. Gomi is very fond of my mother-in-law and minds her well. But people say dogs hide pain, because the one who shows weakness gets left behind, and my mother-in-law is not her person. I cannot prove that is what happened; it is a human way of reading a dog. What I saw was a dog who had been fine all week walk up to me and stop pretending. It hurt to watch, and it felt like being trusted. How dogs conceal pain is covered in 7 subtle signs your dog is in pain.

She had never been spayed, and that was my choice. I believed that spaying a dog very young upsets her hormones and causes bigger trouble later, and I understood pyometra to be something most unspayed dogs never get. My plan was to let her grow up intact and operate if it ever came to that, since it would be surgery either way. I am telling you my reasoning because it is what happened, not because I recommend it. When the plan came due, it meant an operation on an eleven-year-old, the same day, with no time to prepare. Cornell's canine health center says that spaying a dog while she is young and healthy is safer and less costly than an emergency pyometra operation.

The part that went well was the recovery. Gomi had always walked a great deal and stayed fit, and after a general anesthetic and abdominal surgery she came back quickly. I cannot prove the fitness is why. It does fit what the AAHA senior guidelines say, that how well a pet functions before a procedure is the biggest predictor of her quality of life after it. While she recovered I paid close attention to her food and fed her well, and we got her back to short walks as early as we could. She returned to normal.

The second anesthetic was planned: a dental examination with a full set of dental X-rays, and everything came back clear. Same dog, about the same age, with a date on the calendar in place of a same-day emergency. So I no longer ask whether she is too old. I ask whether she is well enough, and whether this is a day we chose.

What to write down before the vet visit

A surgical consult goes better when you arrive with a baseline. These five things are hard to reconstruct afterwards:

  • The change that started this, with the date you first saw it and whether it has grown.
  • A normal day's numbers: how much she eats and drinks, how far she walks, how long she sleeps.
  • Her breathing at rest, counted for one minute while she is asleep.
  • Every medication and supplement, with dose times.
  • Earlier anesthetics, and how she behaved at home in the days after each one.

What only your vet can do

Everything that turns "old" into an actual risk estimate happens in the clinic. The examination and blood work show whether her kidneys, liver, and heart can carry an anesthetic. The health grade pulls those findings into one judgment. The choice of drugs and doses is fitted to her; the senior guidelines note that older animals often need lower doses, adjusted as they go. Monitoring, warming, and fluids during the procedure are the team's work, and so is deciding when she is awake, warm, and comfortable enough to go home.

Pain relief belongs here as well. It comes only from the prescription she is sent home with. Nothing from your own medicine cabinet is safe to add, and that includes human painkillers.

If the answers leave you uneasy, it is reasonable to ask whether a referral hospital with a specialist anesthesia service would change the plan. A veterinarian who is confident in the plan will not mind the question.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 14-year-old dog too old for surgery?

Not because of the number. Veterinary guidelines say advanced age alone is not a reason to rule out general anesthesia. What matters is what the exam and blood work show, what health grade she is given, and whether the operation is planned or an emergency. Weigh that against what happens if the problem is left alone.

If a 15-year-old dog has dental issues, would you have its teeth cleaned?

That depends on the workup, not the birthday. Proper dental treatment needs anesthesia at any age, and senior care guidelines say older animals can have more than one anesthetic when it is needed. Gomi had a dental examination and full dental X-rays under anesthesia at around eleven. The comparison is between a planned anesthetic now and living with a painful mouth.

How old is too old to get your dog spayed?

There is no set cutoff; for an older female the decision rests on her health checks. What changes with age is the alternative. An unspayed female can develop pyometra, a uterine infection treated with emergency surgery, and Cornell's canine health center says spaying a young, healthy dog is safer than that emergency operation. If your older dog is intact, learn the signs: increased thirst, vomiting, listlessness, a swollen belly, or discharge.
