Caring for a senior dog

Senior dog struggling with stairs?

By Gomi's human — written from 11+ years alongside Gomi (@gomithesuperyorkie), not a vet's office · Last updated October 24, 2026 · about a 6-minute read

A new pause at the stairs is worth noticing, but it does not tell you the cause. Pain, weakness, balance problems, and changes in vision can affect how an older dog moves. Record what changed and discuss it with your vet rather than assuming it is simply age or diagnosing arthritis at home.

Part of our complete guide to caring for a senior dog.

Call your vet promptly, not eventually, if: your dog suddenly cannot use a leg at all, drags a paw, cries out when moving, loses balance or falls, or goes from normal to unable to climb within a day or two. A sudden change is a different problem from a gradual one. This article is general information, not a diagnosis.

Why stairs can reveal a change

Climbing and descending place different demands on balance, movement, and weight bearing. A dog who manages a level hallway may still hesitate at a step. Note whether going up, coming down, or both have changed, along with slipping, stumbling, or difficulty getting up from rest.

That pattern is useful information, not a way to locate an injured joint. Do not make your dog repeat a difficult flight to test her. Your vet can assess pain, strength, balance, and other possible causes.

It is probably not "just old age"

Two studies describe different populations and measure different things. Roitner et al. (2024) reviewed radiographs of 6,296 joints from 1,873 dogs aged eight or older at a veterinary teaching hospital. Signs of osteoarthritis appeared in 40.3% of the examined joints, not 40.3% of all older dogs. Importantly, 71.7% of the dogs had presented with orthopedic complaints, so this was not a representative sample of healthy senior dogs.

In a separate UK primary-care population of 455,557 dogs, Anderson et al. (2018) estimated an annual recorded prevalence of appendicular osteoarthritis of 2.5% of dogs. That estimate came from clinical records, not standardized radiographs of every dog.

These percentages cannot be treated as the size of a diagnosis gap. The populations, age selection, units of measurement, and methods differ. Their difference does not show how many cases owners missed, when symptoms began, or why a diagnosis was made later. For an individual dog, the useful next step is to describe the movement change and have a veterinarian assess it.

None of which means your dog definitely has arthritis. It means "she's just old" is a conclusion for your vet to reach after looking, not a reason to skip looking. If you are unsure where your dog sits on the aging curve, our dog age calculator gives you a rough life stage in a few seconds.

What early joint trouble actually looks like

Rarely a limp. Much more often, a shrinking list of things your dog used to do without thinking:

  • A pause at the bottom or top of the stairs before committing.
  • Taking stairs one at a time, or leaning into the wall on the way down.
  • No longer jumping onto the couch, the bed, or into the car — or trying, and aborting.
  • Slipping on hard floors, with legs sliding outward on tile or laminate.
  • Stiffness that fades. Worst on the first walk of the morning or after a long nap, better once she's moving.
  • A changed resting position — sleeping on one side only, or taking longer to settle and to get back up.
  • Licking one joint repeatedly, often a wrist or a knee.
  • Turning around sooner on the usual walk, or lagging on the way home rather than the way out.
  • Being touchy about being lifted, brushed, or handled in one spot.

Weather-sensitive stiffness — noticeably worse on cold or damp mornings — is worth writing down too, because it is a detail your vet can use and one you will not remember accurately three weeks later. Appetite changes can travel with pain as well; if that is happening, our piece on why senior dogs stop eating covers it, and the broader senior dog signs checklist puts mobility alongside everything else worth watching.

Six things you can change at home this week

None of these treat the underlying problem. All of them reduce how much it costs your dog to get through a normal day, and you can do them before the appointment.

  • Put traction where she walks. Runners, yoga mats, or rubber-backed rugs along the hallway and at the top and bottom of the stairs. Slick floors make a dog brace, and bracing hurts.
  • Remove the jumps. A ramp or a set of pet steps at the couch and bed takes out the single highest-impact moment in her day. The landing is the part that does the damage.
  • Raise the food and water bowls. Chest height spares a stiff neck, shoulders, and elbows at every meal.
  • Keep nails short. Long nails change how the paw meets the floor and cost her grip exactly where she needs it.
  • Warm, supportive, easy-in bed. Off the draft, low enough to step into, thick enough that she is not resting a hip on the floor.
  • Two short walks instead of one long one. AAHA's owner guide on mobility suggests exactly that kind of regular, moderate, split activity rather than weekend bursts (AAHA, Mobility Matters).
Do not put your dog on cage rest on your own initiative. Muscle is what holds a sore joint together, and muscle disappears fast in an older dog who stops moving. The aim is gentler movement more often — not less movement.

What only your vet can do

Three things, and they are the three that matter most.

Find out what it actually is. Stair reluctance can be arthritis, but it can also be a disc problem, a cruciate tear, a neurological issue, or referred pain from somewhere else entirely. Those are managed very differently, and telling them apart takes hands on the dog and usually radiographs.

Set a weight target. Weight is the single lever with the largest effect on joint load, and it is free. A vet will give you a number and a plan instead of a guess.

Prescribe pain relief. There is genuinely effective medication for canine joint pain, and it changes dogs' lives. It has to come from a vet.

Never give human painkillers. Ibuprofen, naproxen, and acetaminophen are toxic to dogs and can cause stomach ulcers, kidney failure, or death — including at doses that sound small. If your dog has already had some, call your vet or an animal poison line now.

Write it down before you go

The appointment goes better with specifics. "She's slowed down" gives your vet almost nothing. "Since June she stops at the bottom of the stairs about half the time, it's worse before breakfast and on cold mornings, and she hasn't jumped on the couch since May" gives them a timeline, a pattern, and a starting point.

Start noting the date, which movement she avoided, and the time of day while arranging veterinary advice; do not wait to complete a week of notes. Frequently asked questions

Why is going down harder than going up?

Climbing and descending place different demands on balance, movement, and weight bearing. Hesitation in one direction does not identify which joint hurts or establish that joint pain is the cause. Tell your vet which direction is difficult and whether the change is new.

She's slower, but she doesn't seem to be in pain. Isn't that just aging?

A new change in movement deserves a conversation with your vet rather than an assumption about aging. Pain, weakness, balance problems, and changes in vision are possibilities; a pause at the stairs does not identify the cause.

Do joint supplements help?

Evidence varies a lot by product and by dog, and supplements are not a substitute for a diagnosis or for prescribed pain relief. Ask your vet before adding one, especially if your dog is on other medication.

Should I carry her up and down instead?

For a steep flight or a small dog, carrying can be a reasonable convenience. As a full replacement for movement it backfires, because she loses the muscle that protects the joint. Better to make the stairs safer and keep her using them if your vet agrees she should.
