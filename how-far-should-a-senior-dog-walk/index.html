Caring for a senior dog

How far should a senior dog walk?

By Gomi's human — written from daily life with Gomi, now twelve (@gomithesuperyorkie), not a vet's office · Last updated November 16, 2026 · about an 8-minute read

Your old dog used to pull you to the end of the path, and now she lags on the way home. There is no right distance for a senior dog. The right amount is whatever leaves her moving well the next morning. Split one long walk into two or three short ones, let her choose where to turn around, and if she is stiff the next day, go shorter.

Stop the walk and call the clinic today if: she cannot put weight on a leg or drags a paw; she staggers or collapses; she keeps panting hard after resting in the shade, or her gums look pale, gray, or blue; she suddenly seems confused about where she is; or she stops and cannot move again. This article is general information, not a diagnosis.

Why the walk gets shorter

A shorter walk rarely means she likes walking less. Something has made the same distance cost more, and there are three usual places to look.

Joints, and the pain that comes with them

When older dogs' joints are X-rayed, osteoarthritis is a common finding, as we covered in senior dog struggling with stairs. On flat ground a sore dog can shorten her stride and look almost normal. What she cannot hide is how long she can keep that up, so the walk shrinks: she turns for home sooner or lags on the way back. AAHA's mobility guide for veterinary teams includes a sample owner questionnaire that asks exactly this, whether the dog tires on walks or has become reluctant to walk (AAHA, Mobility Matters). It is one of the easiest signs of pain to put down to age, and 7 subtle signs your dog is in pain covers the others.

Muscle, which goes when she stops

Muscle holds a sore joint steady. A dog who walks less loses muscle, and with less muscle the next walk is harder. The same AAHA guide notes that dogs with later-stage arthritis are often already losing muscle mass, and that exercise matched to the stage of the disease helps fend that loss off. So the answer to a shorter walk is rarely to stop walking. It is to walk differently.

Stamina and weather

An older heart and older lungs have less in reserve, and the AVMA lists tiring quickly and trouble breathing on walks among the reasons to have a dog checked (AVMA, walking with your dog). The AVMA notes that very old dogs have a harder time in the cold, that heat stress can turn into heatstroke quickly, and that hot pavement can burn paw pads.

What you can do this week

None of these change what is causing the slowdown. They change how much each walk costs her, and they give you better information to take to the clinic.

1. Judge the walk by the next morning

You cannot see inside a joint, but you can watch her get up. The morning after a walk, notice three things: how she rises from her bed, how her first few steps look, and whether she pauses before the first stairs or the step out of the door. Then compare that with a morning after a quiet day. If she is stiffer, slower to get going, or hesitating where she did not before, yesterday was more than she could comfortably manage. Go back to the last length that left her moving well, and stay there for a week before you try more.

This works better than counting minutes because it measures the thing you actually care about. Twenty minutes on soft grass in cool air and twenty minutes on hot pavement are different walks. Her next morning tells you which kind it was. On a stiff morning, take a thirty-second phone video of her getting up and walking toward you and away from you; it shows the clinic what a description cannot.

2. Split one long walk into shorter ones

For a dog with sore joints, two or three short walks are usually easier than one long one, even when the total time is the same. For dogs with later-stage arthritis, AAHA's mobility guide describes short sessions of moderate exercise a few times a day, and the AVMA's walking advice starts with short, frequent walks and rests as needed.

If you want a number, ask for one. The same AAHA guide tells veterinary teams to give owners specific walking instructions, such as a set number of minutes twice a day, rather than general advice to exercise more. A number set for your dog, after an exam, beats any number in an article.

3. Let her set the pace and the turnaround

Sniffing is not time wasted. The AVMA recommends letting dogs take sniff breaks, and for an older dog a slow, nose-down walk is real exercise for the body and the mind. Walk at her speed, not yours.

Then remember the arithmetic of an out-and-back walk: the way home is exactly as long as the way out. If you turn around when she is already tired, she has to cover the same distance again on empty legs. Turn while she still wants to go on. A loop that passes close to home halfway round makes this easier, because you can cut it short without a long trek back.

4. Choose the ground, the route, and the time of day

Grass and packed earth are kinder to stiff joints than long stretches of concrete. Avoid what makes her brace: wet boards, polished floors in lobbies, steep slopes, long flights of steps. In summer, walk outside the hottest part of the day, as the AVMA advises, and test the pavement with the back of your hand. In winter, keep to cleared paths; ice and frozen ruts are where stiff legs slip. If her eyes are not what they were, a route she knows well and a time with decent light let her walk with more confidence.

5. Learn the signs that it is time to turn around

Most dogs tell you well before they are exhausted. Turn for home when you see any of these:

  • She drops behind you when she used to walk beside you or ahead.
  • She sits or lies down and needs coaxing to get up.
  • Her head drops and her pace slows, even on ground she likes.
  • She pants harder than the weather explains.
  • She steers toward home at every corner.

A limp, stumbling, or any of the signs in the box at the top of this page are not signs to turn around. They are signs to stop.

6. Plan for rest days and for carrying her home

After a day when she limped, make the next day a short, flat stroll or a rest day with gentle movement around the house and garden. Resting her for weeks on your own initiative works against her, for the muscle reasons above. If a limp is still there after a rest day, or it is getting worse, that is a reason to book a visit, not to rest longer.

If she is small enough to carry, carrying her the last stretch home is not a failure. It lets her enjoy the part of the walk she can manage without paying for the part she cannot. For a larger dog, a shorter loop does the same job.

What we did with Gomi

Gomi and I walk by the river before dawn. She is on the lead almost all the time. One morning when I let her off, she lost track of me for about eight seconds in the half-dark and ran the wrong way until I called her back. That story is in dog dementia signs.

Gomi is twelve now and weighs about eleven pounds. She stops to rest more often than she used to, and some days she limps. These days we go out twice a day for roughly twenty to thirty minutes each time. That is what suits her now, not a target for anyone else's dog. The route is shorter than it was a few years ago, and the turnaround is hers: when she slows and starts looking back toward home, we turn.

After a day she limps, the next walk is short, or we skip it. On some mornings I carry her the last part of the way home. When the weather is bad, we move the walk to a different time of day rather than drop it. The treat comes after the walk, every time.

My best argument for walking came from somewhere unexpected. At eleven, Gomi needed emergency surgery for pyometra, an infected uterus. She recovered quickly. She had always walked a great deal and stayed fit, and I believe that is why. I cannot prove it. It does fit what AAHA's senior care guidelines say: that how well a pet functions before a procedure is "perhaps the biggest predictor" of her quality of life after it. Their example is a healthy, robust fifteen-year-old terrier, expected to recover quickly from having a lump removed (AAHA, 2023). We got her back to short walks as early as we could. The whole story is in is my dog too old for surgery?

So I think of the daily walk as the reserve she draws on when something goes wrong, and I keep it short enough that we can keep it every day.

What to write down before the vet visit

"She doesn't walk as far" is hard to work with. A week of these notes gives the clinic a pattern:

  • How long you walked and roughly where she turned around.
  • How often she stopped, and whether she sat or lay down.
  • The next morning: how she got up, her first steps, and the first stairs.
  • Any limp, with which leg and which day.
  • The weather and the ground, because a cold, wet morning on pavement is a different test from a mild one on grass.

What only your vet can do

Working out why the walk is shrinking happens in the clinic. A hands-on exam, watching her move, and often X-rays can tell a sore joint from a back problem, a torn ligament, or a nerve problem. Listening to her heart and lungs, sometimes with further tests, shows whether stamina is the issue rather than pain. Each is managed differently.

Pain relief belongs here too. Effective pain medication for dogs exists, and it has to be prescribed. Never give human painkillers; several common ones are toxic to dogs. The clinic can also set a weight target, since extra weight adds load to every step, and can refer her for rehabilitation, where swimming, an underwater treadmill, or set exercises are done under a qualified professional.

Frequently asked questions

How far should a senior dog walk? Do they need more rest?

There is no set distance. Judge it by how she moves the next morning: if she gets up stiffly or hesitates at the stairs, yesterday was too much. Two or three short walks usually suit an older dog better than one long one. She does need more rest, but rest should mean gentler movement, not none.

How long is too long of a walk for a dog?

It is too long once she starts lagging, sitting down, panting harder than the weather explains, or steering for home, and whenever a walk leaves her stiff the next day. For an older dog that point comes sooner. The way home is as long as the way out, so turn while she still wants to go on.

How can you help your dog if they are too exhausted to move after a long walk?

Stop, get her into shade or out of the wind, offer water, and carry her if you can. If she cannot stand, staggers, keeps breathing hard after resting, or her gums look pale, gray, or blue, go to the clinic now. Next time, go back to the last length that left her moving well the next morning.
